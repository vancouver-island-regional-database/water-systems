ABSTRACT
      Ladysmith Harbour, supporting a moderate amount of
industrial, commercial, and recreational activities, is
potentially affected in terms of water quality from a number of
sources. One such source is the Ladysmith sewage treatment plant
 (STP) , Presently, the STPs ,. effluent is meeting permit levels
for B.O.D. and T.S.S. However, rainbow trout bioassays indicate
the effluent to be toxic. Ammonia levels, and B.O.D. are likely
key factors contributing to the effluent toxicity. Dissolved
oxygen levels are depressed in the lower depths of the Harbour.
Macoma bioassays indicated toxicity in the sediment, with neither
of the sample sets surviving the test. Continued monitoring
should be performed through out the year. Improving the water
quality of Ladysmith Harbour and removing the shellfish closures
currently in effect are of prime concern.

1.   INTRODUCTION

     The Town of Ladysmith lies within the Cowichan Valley
Regional District and is located along the Trans Canada Highway
adjacent to Ladysmith Harbour. Nanaimo is to the north and
Duncan is on the south. Figure 1 shows the town and treatment
plant site within the region.
     Ladysmith supports a population of approximately 5000. The
economy of Ladysmith has been dominated primarily by industrial,
commercial and recreational development.
     The sewage treatment plant (STP) is located at Holland Bank
on the west shore of Ladysmith harbour. Shoreline homes lie to
the north and several single residences exist to the south.
Ladysmith is located to the northwest, behind the plant site.
     The STP provides primary treatment through screening, grit
removal and collection of settleable solids prior to disinfection
by chlorination and subsequent dechlorination. Screenings and
grit are removed and disposed by landfilling. The sludge is
pasteurized, stabilized and dewatered, and is suitable for
agricultural use. The main building houses the headworks,
autothermophilic aerobic sludge digesters and dewatering process.
The sewage is then transported via pipe to the primary settling
tank, sludge pumping and chlorine contact tank. Two outfalls
combine to convey the final effluent and storm water overflow to
Ladysmith Harbour to a point approximately 810 m offshore at a
discharge depth of about 20 m.
     In the past 10 years, little work has been performed on the
water quality of Ladysmith harbour. Annual testing of the
effluent is completed in regards to the Ladysmith STP permit PE-
120 (Appendix I). Therefore, the present effects of the
discharge upon important resources such as salmonids and
shellfish need to be updated. This report presents information
from two surveys done through the summer of 1993 to gain
knowledge on the immediate or long term impacts, if any, of this
effluent on Ladysmith Harbour. Toxicity and water chemistry were
examined on both the discharge and the receiving environment at 6
sites in Ladysmith Harbour.

2.    SITE DESCRIPTION AND HISTORY
2.1   OCEANOGRAPHY
      Ladysmith Harbour is situated on the southeast coast of
Vancouver Island (Fig. 2) . It is a small inlet which is oriented
in a northwest-southeast direction and has a length of 9 km,
shoreline length of approximately 25 km and surface area of
approximately 1120 ha. The harbour is split into inner and outer
regions via Slag Point, a constriction of the western shoreline
which extends eastward toward the southern tip of Woods Islands.
Stag Point offers protection to the inner harbour while exerting
only minor influences on tidal flushing and circulation (Anon.,
19 81a).
     The inner harbour is characterized by its extensive
intertidal flatlands, shallow water of less than 10 m and greater
variations in water temperature than the outer harbour (McDougall
and Boyd, 19 8 4) . Four creeks drain into the inner harbour
causing variations in surface salinity and water quality
depending on the quantity of freshwater discharged. There are
two sawmills situated on the west shore of the inner harbour.
The harbour provides protection for both intertidal and subtidal
log storage.
     The outer harbour increases in depth to approximately 50 m
and has a larger volume than the inner harbour. As only one
creek drains into the outer harbour, Stuart Channel plays a
greater role on the physical oceanography and to a lesser degree
water from the inner harbour.
     McDougall and Boyd (19 84) discuss a two-layer system of
circulation within Ladysmith Harbour due to the presence of
freshwater. Outflows tend to occur in the upper layers with
inflows occurring in the lower layers for most of the year. As
low density surface water flows out of the harbour, it entrains
more saline water from below developing a slow compensatory
inflow.
      Flushing of the inner harbour area is considered to be poor,
since it is dependent almost exclusively on the exchanging of
water by the tide with some assistance from wind mixing (Anon,
19 81a) . Pomrnen and Holden (19 81) have shown that surface drogues
deployed at the Ladysmith Harbour STP outfall plume can move into
the inner harbour. This movement is accelerated by SE winds,
which tend to blow surfaced effluent towards the head of the
harbour. Thus although the head of the harbour will experience
almost 100% water exchange during a tidal cycle, much of the same
water may return and re-enter the inner harbour.

2.2   FISHER IES RESOUR CE

2.2. 1   Salmonids
     There are five creeks which flow into Ladysmith Harbour:
Bush Creek, Holland Creek, Rocky (Tyee) Creek, Thomas Creek and
Walker Creek (Fig. 2) . Chum (Oncorhynchus keta) and coho (0.
kisutch) salmon utilize all streams flowing into the harbour.
Cutthroat (Salmo clarki) trout are present in all creeks but one,
Bush Creek. Steelhead (S. gairdneri) have been observed in
Holland Creek. Table 1 indicates the historical escapements for
all five creeks (Department of Fisheries and Oceans, 19 8 8) .
      Restricted accessible area has extremely limited the
spawning grounds to the lower reaches in each of the five creeks.
While spawning habitat may be sparse, potential nursery areas for
juvenile salmonids, such as eelgrass and marsh grass beds are
located along the inlets' shoreline. Salt marsh communities exist
in deltas at the mouth of Bush Creek ( 1 40 acres) and Holland
Creek (60 acres) . Tidal drift of insects from the marshes along
with copepods and isopods from the lower mud flats constitute one
of the principal sources of food for rearing salmonids
 (Patterson, 19 75) .

2.2.2    Other Fish Species
     In the 1950's and 60's Ladysmith Harbour was an ideal
location for herring spawn. A survey by Patterson ( 19 75)
revealed that there has been a reduction in the areas utilized by
herring as spawning grounds to only Dunsmuir Island, Sibell Bay
and Sharpe Point. Reduction of eelgrass, rockweed and japweed
beds within the harbour may be one of the major contributing
factors in the failure of herring to spawn within Ladysmith
Harbour.
     Personal communica tion with the DFO offices in Nanaimo
revealed that herring spawn does occur in small numbers, but is
limited to the outer regions of the harbour (ie. Coffin Point) .

closure was lifted there are 11 oyster leases present in
Ladysmith Harbour, each having the opportunity to harvest.
Other Shellfish
     Data on the value of other shellfish resources is very
limited. There is no commercial fishery within the study area
boundaries. It is. known, however, that the harbour supports an
extremely small sports fishery for crabs. The harbour is too
shallow for a shrimp fishery. A sports fishery for clams did
exist on the extensive tidal mud flats at the head of the
harbour. However, due to shellfish closures, no harvesting
currently takes place.

2.3   STP BA CKGROUND

     Prior to 1965 an estimated 1,575,000 L of raw sewage was
discharged daily into the inner harbour via a short outfall pipe
located on north side of Slag Point (Tevendale, 1973a). With the
construction of the primary STP, sewage discharge was diverted
from the inner harbour to the outer harbour while the inner
harbour outfall was converted to handle storm sewer overflows.
     Several surveys in the early 1970's indicated that sewage
contamination from local sources, ie. some sewage lines were
still connected to the storm sewer overflow system and faulty
septic fields, continued to affect much of the inner harbour.
The surveys also revealed deterioration of the water quality in
the outer harbour as a result of diverting most of the town's
sewage to Holland Bank for primary treatment and offshore
discharge.
     In 1974, the STP was upgraded with flow-metering and
chlorination. In addition, faulty septic tank absorption fields
were upgraded in the inner harbour area.
     A 1975 survey ( Cooper and Kay, 1975) found improvements in
water quality in both the inner and outer harbour. However
contamination was still occurring.
     By 19 82 the storm water overflow had been diverted to the
outer harbour and installation of the dechlorination system at
the STP was in effect.
     A report by Kay and Walker (19 85) indicated that discharges
of untreated combined storm and sanitary effluent by way of the
newly constructed bypass outfall into the outer harbour did not
exert a negative impact on the water quality in the Sibell Bay
and Dunsmuir Island area. However the discharge of the sludge
through the STP outfall was found to have a measurable effect on
water quality in the Sibell Bay area.
     In 19 8 8 autothermophilic aerobic sludge processing
facilities were added. This was to stop further sludge discharge
into Ladysmith Harbour.

re quirements for the effluent quality at the plant. Overflow
patterns for past years indicated low to nil values during the
dry months (May - October) and excessive values during the wet
months (November-April). These overflow effluent disharges
exceed the permitted values on daily and weekly periods during
wet weather. Dayton and Knight (1991) proposed two options
dealing with these overflow values and the expansion of the

3.   MATERIALS AND METHODS

     Samples were collected f-rom a number of locations in and
around Ladysmith Harbour on May 20 and July 6, 1993. The tides
and time of sampling are as follows: May 20 from 1100 to 1255
hrs (pst) with a flood tide low at 11 40 hrs; July 6 from 08 40 to
1055 hrs (pst) with an ebb tide high at 0655 hrs. Figure 3
indicates the position of each site and nature of the sampling.
     Marine water samples were collected at just below the
surface at all sites by immersing the appropriate collection
bottle. Other samples were taken at various depths for each
station by using a Van Dorn water collection bottle. All samples
were subsequently shipped in coolers to Zenon Environmental in
Burnaby for analysis, as outlined in Clark and Shera (1985) . A
summary of the water quality tests for each of the stations is
listed in Table 3.
     At each of the marine stations, a variety of parameters were
measured through out the water column using a Hydro Lab Surveyor
I I. These included temperature, dissolved oxygen (DO) , pH,
oxidation reduction potential (ORP) and salinity. Results of
these physical oceanographic measurements are presented in
Appendix I I.
     Bottom sediments were obtained using an Eckman grab.
Numerous grabs (30- 40) were made at each site to obtain
sufficient material for analysis (approx. 1/ 4 to 1/3 cu ft) . The
sediment samples were shipped to the Aquatic Toxicity Lab for
Macoma and Microtox bioassays. Subsamples of approximately 500
ml were sent to Zenon Labs for a modified SWEP (salt water
extraction process) analysis, upon which an Echinoderm
fertilization bioassay was performed.

4.    EFFLUENT CHARACTERISTICS

4.1   Water Quality
      The final effluent was sampled twice during the summer of
1993 (May 20 and July 8). As shown in Table 4, the B.O.D. and
T.S.S. were well within their permit limit of 130 mg/ L with
B.O.D. values of 6 7 and 70 mg/1 and T.S.S. values of 30 and 1 7
mg/ L for each respective sampling date. B.O.D. values were
always relatively higher than T.S.S. values. Previous data
collected yearly for the STP effluent indicate B.O.D. and T.S.S.
values of under the 130 mg/ L permit limit (Appendix I).
Exceptions occur in October, 19 8 8 and September, 1990 with B.O.D.
values of 1 40 and 136 mg/ L respectively.

initial sample had low ammonia (less than 0.005 mg/L and high

ammonia is being broken down in the STP system, resulting in
higher organic nitrogen values. The second set of results
however were quite the opposite with high ammonia of 23.2 mg/ L
and lower organic nitrogen of 7. 80 mg/L. This suggests that the
ammonia is not properly being broken down. There are a number of
possibilities which could lead to these values such as water flow
levels, sludge removal, etc.. McDougall and Boyd (19 8 4) suggest
that lack of storm water dilution would likely result in higher
mean values of ammonia. Two sets of sample data seems

4.2   Toxicity of Effluent
       A bioassay for acute rainbow trout toxicity (96hr LC50) was
performed on the July 8th sample. The rainbow trout bioassay
indicated an LC50 of 7 4. 8%. A previous rainbow trout bioassay
sample taken on September 30, 1991 had the same LC50 value of
7 4. 8%. Even though both dates indicate the same LC50 value it is
somewhat biased to compare them as different factors could have
lead to the resulted value. For example B.O.D. levels for July
1993 and September 1991 were different at 70 and 116 mg/L
respectively. Ammonia levels were relatively higher for July
1993 sample but alas unknown for September 1991. For comparative
purposes, a chinook bioassay was run on the July 8th sample. This
resulted in a 96hr LC50 value of less than 33%, obviously highly
toxic. It appears that chinook were more sensitive to the final
effluent than were rainbow trout.
     For the May 20th sample, not enough effluent was collected
to perform the 96hr LC50, however an LT50 (lethal time to kill
50% of the sample size) at 100% concentration was carried out on
rainbow trout. This resulted in a value of 4 8 hrs. An LT50 value
of less than 2 4 hrs was extrapolated from the July 8 data.
     Overall it could be said that the effluent has remained
relatively stable but somewhat toxic. The July toxicity is likely
due to the presence of high ammonia. However, it should be noted
that the practical difficulty of sampling the effluent further
"downstream" of the dechlorination point, may have resulted in a
poorly mixed sample and a high chlorine level. However, sample
aeration prior to and during the bioassay procedure should
rapidly dissipate the chlorine.

5.     Receiving Environment
     All pertinent receiving environment data for water chemistry
is presented in Tables 5 and 6. Appendix I I contains the
hydrolab depth profiles for each of the six sites in the harbour.
5. 1     Marine Water
5. 1.1    Physical Oceanography
       Temperatures for the May sample data ranged from 8.52 to
1 7. 25 C while the July temperatures were slightly warmer ranging
from 9. 8 8 to 1 8.13 C (Appendix I I). As to be expected warmer
waters were found at the head and cooler waters at the mouth of
the harbour. This condition apparently results from the large
intertidal zone acting as a heat-sink during low tide periods,
thus heat is subse quently transferred to overlying water during
flood tides in the inner harbour (McDougal and Boyd, 19 84).
Outer harbour temperatures are lower due to a greater degree of
mixing with colder water from Stuart Channel.
     Salinities recorded in May ranged from 25. 80 to 2 8. 90 ppt
while those in July ranged from 25.10 to 2 8.60 ppt (Appendix I I).
The second sampling data overall had lower salinity values. The
reduced surface salinites suggest the freshwater influence of
Thomas, Walker, Bush, Rocky and Holland Creeks (McDougall and
Boyd, 19 84).
5. 1.2     Dissolved Oxygen
      The maintenance of dissolved oxygen concentrations is
essential for the protection of aquatic life. Significant stress
can be placed upon organisms through the combination of low
dissolved oxygen concentrations and toxic chemicals ( Davis,
19 75). The toxicity of some substances, such as ammonia, are
enhanced by low dissolved oxygen. As well, higher water
temperatures increase the adverse effects of low dissolved oxygen
on fish.
      Monitoring on May 20, 1993 indicated dissolved oxygen
concentrations ranging from 7.0 8 to 14. 00 mg/L (Appendix I I).
Most values were between 9. 00 to 12.00 mg/ L. By July 8, 1993 the
dissolved oxygen tended to be approximately 3 to 4 mg/ L lower,
ranging from 4. 66 to 10. 43 mg/L. Most values were between 6 to 8
mg/ L. Cooler water temperatures in May play a role in the higher
DO values observed.
     Dissolved oxygen concentrations generally decreased as the
depth increased. Exceptions occurred around the 4 m level where
an increase in DO was observed. This could be due to the
presence of the two-layer circulation system found in Ladysmith
Harbour where outflows occurr in the upper layers and inflows in

--- Page 21 ---

                                 17
the lower layers. A phytoplankton maxima around that depth could
also have lead to the increase in dissolved oxygen. Due to poor
flushing of the inner harbour, DO values in July tended to be
quite low.
     The approved and working criteria for water quality
recommends 6.5 mg/ L of DO for aquatic marine life (Pommen, 19 89) .
Presently only the upper portions of Ladysmith Harbours' water
column meets this recommendation. As to how much the STP effluent
contributes to the decrease in DO at lower depths is
indeterminable. There are other factors in the harbour which play
a role in lowering the DO. Logging and mud flats located in the
inner harbour have some influence. During summer months
circulation is poor, due to calm winds, and less freshwater is
drained into the harbour. Collectively, these factors plus
increased water temperature all work together to decrease the
dissolved oxygen present in Ladysmith Harbour.
5. 1.3   Nitrogen
     Nitrate, nitrite and ammonia are the major inorganic
nitrogen compounds occuring in surface waters. Two factors, pH
and temperature of the water, are important to the toxicity of
ammonia (Nordin and Pommen, 19 86) . Therefore in the receiving
environment, the principle effects of ammonia input are: a)
excessive ammonia levels can be toxic to aquatic life; b)
nitrification of excessive ammonia input will utilize substantial
portions of dissolved oxygen; c) input of ammonia may lead to
nuisance phytoplankton blooms, which as part of their seasonal
cycle may lead to depressed dissolved oxygen. Organic nitrogen
in high levels will over time decompose, potentially leading to
oxygen depletion.
      At sites 4 and 5 located on either side of the STP
discharge, the nitrogen data appears somewhat inconsistent (Table
5) . This may be a reflection of the variation of ammonia and
organic nitrogen results from the STP effluent. Organic nitrogen
levels at site 4 remained relatively similar with only slight
fluctuations (0. 10 to 0. 15 mg/ L) . However for site 5, organic
nitrogen levels in July (0.0 4 mg/ L) decreased by approximately 5
times those of May (0.20 mg/ L) . Ammonia levels at site 4 were
higher for July data than May. At the surface in May ammonia was
virtually undetectable (less than 0.005 mg/L) , however in July
surface levels were 0.0 19 mg/ L. The site 5 ammonia levels varied
as well. The May data was consistent with site 4 in that low
levels were found at the surface (0.005 mg/L) and higher levels
found at the bottom (0.099 mg/L) . The July data had its' lowest
ammonia value of less than 0.005 at the bottom ( 1 8.0 m) , it then
increased to 0.025 mg/L at 10.0 m and finally lowered to 0.016
mg/ L at the surface. These results could be a reflection of the
STP effluent data where in July the organic nitrogen level and
ammonia level were opposite than the May data.

     Fecal coliform and enterococci levels remained virtually

5.2   Marine Sediment
     Sediment samples were obtained on both dates, however the
May sample was taken on a trial basis and limited to a Microtox
bioassay only. The July sediment sample was expanded to include
other bioassays. The sediment samples, taken from either side of
the STP outfall, had a distinct hydrogen sulphide odour and were
black in color. It should be noted that the comparison sediment
taken from outside Ladysmith Harbour near Coffin Point also had a
strong hydrogen sulphide odour.
     Microtox is a marine strain of bioluminescence photo
bacteria which emits a blue green light as a natural function.
When challenged by a toxicant, a decrease in light output is
measured and an E C50 (effective concentration causing 50% light
decrease) is recorded. The Microtox bioassay results from
Ladysmith Harbour A and B sites for both sample dates indicated
no significant toxicity to the bacteria (greater than 1% EC50) .
The results ranged from a minimum value of 2.5 7% (Ladysmith
Harbour B, May 20) to a maximum value of 10.95% (Ladysmith
Harbour A, July 8) . The control sample could not be computed due
to the sediment particle size.

      The echinoderm fertilization bioassay first requires a
modified SWE P to be performed on the sediment samples. This
process uses salt water to extract any toxicants which happen to
be in the sediment. Echinoderm gametes are then exposed to this
extraction of various concentrations, incubated and mixed with
eggs. After a period of incubation the eggs are studied to
record percent fertilization. This bioassay is also ideal for
determining receiving environmental impacts. The bioassay was
conducted at 100% concentration of the modified SWE P. The
control experiment resulted in 95% fertilization of the
echinoderm gametes. Site B and site C showed similar results with
8 8% and 86% fertilization respectively, indicating minimal
toxicity. Due to variations in the lab results for Site A, the
test is currently being reconducted.

6.   CONCLUSIONS

     At present B. O.D. and T. S.S. values are well within their
permitted limit for the Ladysmith ST P effluent, however rainbow
trout bioassays indicate that the effluent is acutely toxic. Of
concern was the alternating high value of ammonia and organic
nitrogen between the two sample dates.
     Water quality in the harbour indicates that DO levels may be
be affected. Dissolved oxygen concentrations are currently below
the acceptable standards in the lower depths of the water column.
However, in addition to the ST P, there are a number of other
factors which may contribute to depressed dissolved oxygen
levels, such as log booming, inner harbour mud flats and other
natural phenomena. Monitoring should be conducted throughout the
year to determine DO levels at various seasons particularly
through the late summer/fall. Circulation will increase in the
harbour with winter storms as well as increased influence from
the freshwater creeks which ultimately will bring DO levels back

up.
     Bacteriological measurements during low flow levels seems to
indicate minimal contamination is taking place in the harbour.
Follow up surveys should be done to see what happens when flows
increase at the STP. Will fecal coliform numbers increase with
the excessive storm water entering the effluent?
     There is indication of long term effects from Ladysmith S TP
effluent on the receiving environment. Sediment samples taken
within the immediate vicinity of the outfall appear toxic. The
toxicity of the sediment appears likely due to the build up of
hydrogen sulphide. During sampling it was noted that the
sediment was black within several centimeters of the surface and
emitted a strong hydrogen sulfide odour. The Macoma bioassay had
no living clams after the test period which is also a strong
indication of the toxicity present in the sediment. The sediment
deposition, however, may be attributable to historic sludge
disposal procedures (i.e. through outfall) . Further sediment
analysis of parameters such as metals is recommended.
Furthermore in monitoring the sediment, samples $hould be taken
in a gradient beginning at the outfall and moving outward. This
would indicate the level of toxicity and at what point the
sediment becomes non-toxic. Perhaps divers could be used to
inspect more restricted areas. Monitoring should be continued so
as to find and record any changes that may occur.
     While the results presented here are somewhat preliminary in
nature, they are nevertheless of concern. Even though B.0.D.
levels meet the pres,ent STP permit requirements, they may
nevertheless remain high enough to impact the receiving
environment, contributing to the apparent reduction of dissolved
oxygen in the receivng environment and sediment toxicity through
the build-up of hydrogen sulphide. The effluent continues to be
toxic likely as a result of high B.O.D. levels and fluctuating
ammonia levels. Infiltration continues to be a problem through
the winter months leading to shellfish harvesting closures.
Reducing present B.O.D. loadings, ammonia levels and dealing with
the infiltration problem are the key to removing the shellfish
closures and producing a non-toxic effluent. The two options
presented for improving the Ladysmith sewage treatment plant
should be further'investigated.
