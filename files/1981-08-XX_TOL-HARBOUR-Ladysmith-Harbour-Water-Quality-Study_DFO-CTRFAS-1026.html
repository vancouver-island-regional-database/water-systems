            A bioassay using Pacific oyster Crassostrea gigas, Thunberg ,
emb r yos was done on surface waters collected from a depth of 0.5 m at fiv e
locations from the mouth to the head of Ladysmith Harbour, British Columbia
from January 8 to September 17, 1979. Water quality as determi ned by the
bioassay was poorer in Ladysmith Harbour water than in control wate r at the
Pacific Biological Station in Nanaimo. There was a gradient in water
quality in the harbour from the mouth, where it was best, to the head, where
it was poorest but local exceptions to this trend were evident. Water
samples were collected in 1979 and 1980 and analysed by gas
chromatograph-mass spectrometry to identify the possible causes of the poo r
water quality. No definitive cause was identified but wood extractives,
chlorophenols, fuel additives and dimethyl sulphide, all of which can be
toxic to oyster larvae, were found. Six samples of water were collected
from June 26 to September 13, 1979 and analysed for copper, mercury and
arsenic content but concentrations of these metals were too low to explain
the poor water quality.

             spectrometry, chlorophenols, wood extractives.

                                INTRODUCTION



          Ladysmith Harbour is a n ar row inlet on the lower east side of
Vancouver Island approximately 8 km in length and varying in width from 0.7
to 2 km (Fig. 1). The harbour is divided into an inner shallower portion
and an outer wider deeper part by a constriction approximately in the
centre. There are extensive intertidal flats in the upper part of the inner
harbour.  Maximum water depth in the inner harbour is about 9 m and in th e
outer harbour about 37 m.

            The harbour has been an important oyster producing area for many
years . Originally native oysters Ostrea lurida Carpenter, were harvested
there. Eastern oysters, Crassostrea virginica Gmelin, were introduced
around the turn of the century but the transfer was not successful and the
practice was discontinued.    In 1912 or 1913 the Pacific oyster, ~~ _&~&~~
Thunberg, was introduced into the harbour and this started a flourishing
industry. By 1925 limited Pacific oyster breeding had occurred and
s ig nificant introductions of seed from Japan began in that year and
continued for many years . Limited breeding was reported in 1926, 1930 and
1931 and the first major breeding occurred in 1932 (Quayle 1969). This was
the beginning of a series of breedings of some magnitude in the harbour.
Another major breeding occurred in 1936. The harbour became an important
oyster growing area and in 1949 the British Columbia Provincial government
established an oyster research laboratory there where studies on oysters and
oyster culture were carried out until the facility was closed in 1957.

            Oyster harvesting in the harbour ceased in June 1965 when it was
closed to harvesting because of sewage contamination (Cooper and Kay 1975).
An improvement was made in sewage treatment by the town of Ladysmith and, as
a r es ult of further water quality surveys in 1970 and 1975, part of the
inner harbour was re~opened for direct culture and harvesting of oysters.
All the outer portion of the harbour remained closed .

          Despite the re ~openin g of much of the oyster growing area of the
inner harbour, oyster production from Ladysmith Harbour declined and has
virtually ceased . This was blamed on a deterior ation in water quality that
affected oyster growth and fatness as well as breeding.    Breeding was
particularly affected .  The last commercial set of Pacific oysters in
Ladysmith Harbour (mean of 10 spat per piece of Pacific oyster shell cult c h)
occurred in 1971 (Bourne 1978) although light scattered breeding occurred in
some years since then.   Hydrographic conditions since 1971 have been
favorable in some years to produce a commercial set in the harbour
(particularly 1975, 1977 and 1978). Spawning was observed and sufficient
quantities of larvae to the early umbone stage were found in plankton to
produce a commercial spatfall but none occurred (Bourne, unpublished MS;
Heritage and Bourne 1979) (Table 1).

          A further indication of the degradation of water quality was an
apparent decline in the faunal species of the inner part of the harbour
(D. B. Quayle, pers . comm.). Changes in the aquatic community structure are
a well known indicator of altered wate r quality (Cairns and Dickson 1971;
Cairns et al. 1972; Leathem et al. 1973). Most forms of stress reduce the
complexity of aquatic ecosystems (Cairns and Dickson 1971; Boesch 1972).

          In November 1973 the Provincial Lan,j Management Branch, Department

Ladysmith Harbour (Anon 1976). This comprehensive study of the harbour

proposed guidelines for consideration for future management. A necessary

an oyster embryo bioassay project to assess water quality in the harbour.
The project was carried out at the Pacific Biological Station in Nanaimo and
began in September 1978 and terminated in December 1979 . The bioassay would
indicate the degree of water degradation but would not determine the source
of the problem.  Consequently arra ngements we re made with the West Vancouver
Laboratory to undertake analyses of water samples from the harbour for
organic compounds that could be the source of any water quality degradation.

water degradation. This project began in May and terminat ed in August and
all analyses of water samples we re again made at the West Vancouver
Laboratory.




                         OYSTER EMBRYO BIOASSAY METHOD




          The oyster or bivalve embryo bioassay technique has been used
extensively to assess water quality in estuarine waters, (Anon 1980;
Loosanoff and Davis 1963; Dimick and Breese 1965; and Woelke 1972).     It is a
sensitive and rapid indicator of water quality because developing bivalve
embryos and larvae respond quickly to minor quantities of pollutants.
During the initial 48-hour period of development, fertilized eggs normally
dev;lop into free-swimming, fully shelled, veli ger larvae.  Failure to
develop into normal larvae or developing in an abnormal manner is used as a n
indicator of polluted or stressful conditions.   The principal c riterion has
beel a comparison of th e percentage of normal larvae found in control a nd in
test waters 48 h after fertili z ation. A further advantage of the test is

that embryos of many bivalves can be obtained on demand. The detailed
method of the bioassay has been described in the above references but a
brief outline follows.

           During the spawning season adult oysters can be readily spawned in
the laboratory.   Out of the spawning season, adult bivalves are conditioned
by gradually raising the water temperature until spawning temperatures are
attained and then holding them at this temperature for 4-6 weeks until the
g onads become mature. When embryos are required, adults are spawned by
rapidly raising and lowering the water temperature at half-hour intervals in
spawning dishes. The addition of a small amount of sperm extract is
frequently used to initiate spawning if this is not achieved by temperature
manipulations alone.

          The spawned eggs are washed and filtered to remove pieces of
tissue, resuspended in clean salt water and fertilized by adding small
amounts of sperm. The number of zygotes is counted and a known number added
to the test and control waters.  Cultures are held for 48 hours at
incubating temperatures which depend on the species of bivalve used; for
Pacific oysters the temperature is usually 25°C.   After 48 hours the larvae
are filtered, resuspended in salt water and examined microscopically. A
measure of water quality is obtained by comparing the number of normal
veliger larvae with the total number of larvae or by counting the number of
normal larvae that developed from the number of embryos in the control and
test waters.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION


          Twenty-one bioassays were carried out on Ladysmith Harbour waters
during the period January 8 to September 17, 1979 (Table 2). Results of
these bioassays are summarized in Fig. 2-12.

          The commonest type of abnormal larvae observed in the bioassays
were those with misshapen valves; the ventral margin was crinkled or uneven,
evoluted outward, and in some instances notched.  Larvae with these
conditions could not close the valves properly. Frequently the tissues of
abnormal larvae were withdrawn and did not extend to the ventral margin of
the shells. Tissues of some larvae were extruded and could not be withdrawn
into the valves.  The velum of some larvae was misshapen and did not extend
from the margin of the valves. Hith these and other larvae, swimming was
atypical and the larvae were obviously abnormal.  In some instances very
abnormal larvae developed (so called ear muffed or black spot). Another
criterion was the colour of the larvae; normal larvae are brown in colour
with a pinkish tinge, abnormal larvae lacked the pinkish tinge and were pale
and washed out.

           In most of the bioassays the number of larvae that developed in
the 48-hour period and the percentage of normal larvae were higher in the
control than in the test samples. There were two exceptions, those of
January 22 and February 19. In both these bioassays the thermoregulator
controlling the temperature in the bath malfunctioned and water temperatures
varied from 15 to 31°C during the 48-hour period. After the February 19
bioassay all beakers were kept in a bath that was surrounded by water from
the heated sea water system of the Pacific Biological Station. A further
complication in the January 22 bioassay was that insufficient eggs were
obtained from spawning adults and eggs were stripped from adult females.
Results of these two bioassays may not be comparable with those of other
bioassays.

          Some immediate steps could be undertaken to alleviate the state of

          A detailed oceanographic study of the harbour is needed with
particular emphasis on currents both at the surface and at depth. Current
pattern studies from the sewage outfall should be expanded to determine what
effect this water has on other parts of the harbour. Oxygen levels at
various depths should be measured over a period of time, particularly during
summer when thermal stratification may occur, to determine if oxygen
depletion occurs at depth.   If oxygen depletion occurs, the influence of
this water on surface waters should be assessed.   Stress caused by the
presence of sublethal levels of organic toxicants might result in
mortalities in oxygen-depleted sea water.

          The degree of toxicity of chemicals found in the present study on
oyster larvae should be measured. A particularly important investigation
would be a study of substances leaching from logs stored in sea water and
their effect on oyster larvae, adult oysters and other marine organisms.
This has far reaching implications since logs are stored in a variety of
sites along the British Columbia coast and it is important to know the
effect of this storage on the marine ecosystem.

          The organic chemical analyses should be expanded and carried out
on a routine basis.  Quantification of chemicals in the harbour waters that
are toxic to oyster larvae should be undertaken with particular emphasis on
wood leachates. Derivatives of marine fuel products might cause some degree
of pollution and should be investigated. Regular monitoring of the sewage
outfall would indicate if significant quantities of toxic substances are
continuously or intermitte n tly entering the harbour. The BOD of the sewage
should be determined since this might be critical during the warmer weather
months when stratification occurs. Chemical studies of the sediment,
particularly in the deeper areas, would show if any toxic materials are
being incorporated there. The amount of chlorophenols in liver tissue of
marine animals such as sculpins should be measured to determine if levels in
the harbour are higher or lowe r than in other localities in the Province.

          Ladysmith Harbour was an important oyster producing area and it is
highly desirable that it return to its former levels of production. The
amount of bottom suitable for intertidal oyst er culture in southern British
Columbia is limited to about 1,000 hectares (Quayle 1969) of which about 10%
is in the Ladysm it h Harbour area. Loss of this ground would be a serious
impediment to the British Columbia oyster industry in its efforts to attain
former levels of production.

          An important question is, if the causes of pollution were
immediately removed, would Ladysmith Harbour recover to permit the former
type of mar ine community and if so how long would it require. No definite
answer can be given but it should not prevent efforts to identify the causes
of pollution in Ladysmith Harbour and to remove them to enable the harbour
waters to recover and permit normal levels of oyster production.
